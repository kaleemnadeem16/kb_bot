## What machine learning is

Machine learning (ML) lets a computer find patterns in data and use them to make predictions or decisions, without a programmer writing the rules by hand. You collect examples, train a model on them, then check how well it does on examples it has not seen.

## Three common styles

### Supervised learning
In supervised learning every training example comes with the right answer, called a label. The model learns to map inputs to labels and is then used on new inputs. Two typical tasks:
- Classification: choose a category, for example spam or not spam.
- Regression: predict a number, for example the price of a house.

### Unsupervised learning
Here there are no labels. The model looks for structure on its own:
- Clustering: grouping similar items, such as customers with similar buying habits.
- Association: finding things that tend to occur together, such as products bought in the same basket.

### Reinforcement learning
An agent tries actions in an environment and gets rewards or penalties. Over many attempts it learns which behaviour earns the most reward. Game-playing programs and robot control are classic examples.

## Typical uses

- **Healthcare**: spotting disease in images and predicting patient risk.
- **Finance**: detecting fraud and scoring credit applications.
- **Technology**: search ranking and product recommendations.
- **Automotive**: lane detection and predicting when a part needs maintenance.

## Well-known algorithms

- **Linear regression**: fits a straight-line relationship to predict a number.
- **Decision trees**: split the data with a series of yes/no questions.
- **Random forest**: averages many decision trees to get steadier answers.
- **Support vector machines**: find the boundary that separates classes with the widest margin.
- **Neural networks**: layers of simple connected units that can learn complex patterns.

## Practical advice

Good results depend more on clean, representative data than on the choice of algorithm. Always hold back some data for testing, and compare against a simple baseline before trusting a complicated model.
